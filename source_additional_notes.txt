Social in Interactions: Defined by observed in-game actions, verbal/non-verbal communication, or external interactions beyond gameplay (including spectators)

Thesaurus of Social Gaming Operationalizations
This thesaurus systematizes the multi-disciplinary operationalizations of "social gaming", "social play", and "in-game social interaction" across cyberpsychology, Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), media studies, behavioral medicine, and clinical psychology.
Rather than a unified monolithic construct, the literature operationalizes social gaming across five overarching operational clusters:
Platform-Embedded Social Network Games (SNGs & Mobile SNGs)
Social Casino Games (SCGs) and Simulated Gambling
Psychosocial Motivations, Social Capital, and Clinical Multiplayer Gaming
HCI Interaction Design, Spatial Sociality, and Developmental Affordances
Empirical Interventions, Tabletop Co-Play, and Assistive Social Playfulness

1. Platform-Embedded Social Network Gaming
Headword: Social Network Game (SNG / Mobile SNG)
SN: Video game software integrated structurally with Social Network Services (SNS) platforms and mobile operating systems, utilizing pre-existing real-world social graphs to drive player acquisition, viral loops, and ongoing retention.
UF: Asynchronous Social Gaming; Casual Social Gaming; Social Platform Games; Connective Commodity Games; Mobile Social Network Games (MSNGs).
BT: Digital Games; Social Media Services.
NT: Asynchronous Gifting; Leaderboard Benchmarking; Life/Energy Request Loops; Social Invites.
RT: Freemium Business Models; Microtransactions; Time-Gated Progression; Platform Capitalism.
OP (Operational Indicators):
Social Graph Activation: Number of external platform friends connected; invitations sent to non-playing platform contacts.
Asynchronous Resource Exchange: Frequency of daily gifting (sending/receiving "lives", "energy", extra moves); fulfillment of peer help requests.
Social Competition Telemetry: Asynchronous leaderboard rank tracking; frequency of inspecting friends' stage progression on a shared map interface.
Socio-Commercial Behaviors: Frequency and monetary volume of in-app microtransactions to bypass social wait-gates or fulfill social gift reciprocity.
IN (Measurement Instruments):
Extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) & UTAUT2 inventories (measuring Social Influence, Subjective Norms).
Flow Experience Scales (measuring Perceived Enjoyment, Concentration, and Telepresence).
Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT) Questionnaires (social inclusion and pastime motives).
CS (Core Studies): Wang & Li (2020); Nieborg (2015, Crushing Candy); Al-Emran et al.; Abbasi et al.; Holebeek et al.
2. Simulated Gambling and Virtual Risk-Taking
Headword: Social Casino Game (SCG)
SN: Free-to-play digital gaming applications accessible via social media or mobile apps that visually, acoustically, and structurally replicate casino gambling (slots, poker, roulette) without paying out real-world cash prizes.
UF: Simulated Gambling; Gambling-Like Social Gaming; Non-Monetary Casino Gaming; Converged Social Gaming.
BT: Free-to-Play Video Games; Gambling Media.
NT: Social Slots; Social Poker; Social Sports Betting; Virtual Currency Simulators.
RT: Monetization Convergence; Microtransactions; Loot Boxes; Real-Money Gambling Migration.
OP (Operational Indicators):
Simulated Gambling Volume: Daily/weekly session frequency, minutes spent per session, and total lifetime plays of slot or card simulations.
Virtual Financial Outlay: Real currency expenditure dedicated to purchasing non-cashable virtual credits/coins.
Platform Dissemination: Engagement with social casino advertisements targeted via behavioral advertising on social media feeds.
Algorithmic Exposure: Player exposure to dynamic game balancing (artificially inflated payout odds compared to commercial gambling mathematics).
Migration / Gateway Metric: Percentage of players initiating cash-based gambling on commercial gambling sites following exposure to SCGs.
IN (Measurement Instruments):
Gambling Related Cognitions Scale (GRCS) adapted for simulated contexts (illusions of control, predictive control).
South Oaks Gambling Screen (SOGS) / Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) applied to track cross-migration.
Longitudinal Behavioral Tracking and Transactional Ledger Logging.
CS (Core Studies): Gainsbury et al. (2014, 2016); King & Delfabbro (2016); Kim et al. (2016, 2017); Abarbanel & Gainsbury (2020).
3. Psychosocial Motivations & Online Social Capital
Headword: Social Gaming Motivation
SN: The psychological drive to initiate and sustain digital gameplay for the purpose of interpersonal interaction, communication, community belonging, and collective achievement.
UF: Player Social Orientation; In-Game Interpersonal Drive; Social Play Motives.
BT: Motivation to Game; Self-Determination Theory (Relatedness).
NT:
Socializing Motive: The desire to engage in casual conversation, meet new people, and exchange banter.
Relationship Motive: The drive to establish deep, meaningful, and emotionally intimate personal bonds with in-game contacts.
Teamwork Motive: The orientation toward coordinated, collaborative group play, mutual assistance, and shared objective achievement.
RT: Escapism; Achievement Motive; Immersion; Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD).
OP (Operational Indicators):
Standardized psychometric Likert scale responses assessing social desire versus achievement/escapism drives.
Self-reported prioritization of guild/clan attendance and collaborative matches over individual gameplay.
IN (Measurement Instruments):
Yee’s Motivation of Play Inventory (isolating the Social Component and subcomponents: Socializing, Relationship, Teamwork).
Motives for Online Gaming Questionnaire (MOGQ — Social Motive subscale).
CS (Core Studies): Yee (2006); Wang & Cheng (2022) / Cheng et al. (2022); Billieux et al.
Headword: In-Game Social Capital
SN: The accumulation of actual or potential psychosocial resources embedded within, available through, and derived from a player's network of digital gaming contacts.
UF: Virtual Social Capital; Online Gamer Network Resources.
BT: Social Capital; Network Theory.
NT:
Bridging In-Game Capital: Outward-looking networks composed of diverse, heterogeneous, weak ties that provide exposure to novel perspectives, information, and broad community acceptance.
Bonding In-Game Capital: Inward-looking networks characterized by strong, emotionally intimate ties providing substantive emotional support, psychological safety, and reciprocal loyalty.
RT: Loneliness; Offline Social Support; Social Network Analysis (Centrality, Density); Social Alienation.
OP (Operational Indicators):
Structural network metrics: In-degree/out-degree centrality within game guilds; sociometric density of gamer friend networks.
Perceived resource availability: Likert ratings evaluating the perceived presence of peers who can provide emotional comfort (bonding) or access to new resources/perspectives (bridging).
Compensatory index: Ratio of online social capital to offline network support in alienated individuals.
IN (Measurement Instruments):
Williams’ Internet Social Capital Scale (ISCS — Online Bridging and Online Bonding subscales).
Social Network Analysis (SNA) graph extraction (Gephi / NodeXL network modeling of in-game guilds).
CS (Core Studies): Domahidi (2018); Xian et al. (2020); Shen & Chen (2015); Trepte, Reinecke, & Juechems (2012).
Headword: Disordered Social Obligation
SN: The clinical operationalization wherein in-game social relationships and clan expectations transform into rigid, compulsory interpersonal demands that override self-regulation and sustain problematic gaming behavior.
UF: Clan Pressure; Guild Obligation Compulsion; Pathological In-Game Loyalty.
BT: Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD); Behavioral Addiction.
RT: Fear of Missing Out (FoMO); Synchronous Raiding; Relationship Motive Vulnerability.
OP (Operational Indicators):
Self-reported guilt or anxiety experienced when unable to log in for scheduled team matches or raids.
Continued gameplay despite acute negative life consequences (academic failure, occupational impairment, sleep deprivation) explicitly justified by a duty to avoid letting team members down.
IN (Measurement Instruments):
Lemmens Internet Gaming Disorder Scale (IGD-9).
Ten-Item Internet Gaming Disorder Test (IGDT-10).
DSM-5 / ICD-11 Diagnostic Symptom Criteria (Criteria: Loss of control, Prioritization of gaming over life interests, Continued use despite negative consequences).
CS (Core Studies): Wang & Cheng (2022); Stevens et al. (2021); Ho et al. (2025).
4. HCI Architecture, Spatiality & Developmental Affordances
Headword: Social Presence in Gaming (Kaye’s 2×2 Matrix)
SN: A structural taxonomy operationalizing social gaming along two intersecting continuum axes: the physical/virtual nature of player presence and the degree of behavioral/task interdependence.
UF: Spatial-Behavioral Game Classification; Structural Game Sociality.
BT: Human-Computer Interaction (HCI); Social Presence Theory.
NT:
Physical / High Interdependence: Co-located direct cooperative/competitive play ("couch multiplayer", split-screen console play, tabletop).
Physical / Low Interdependence: Co-located parallel play ("couch co-presence", playing independent mobile/handheld games in the same physical room).
Virtual / High Interdependence: Online synchronous cooperative play (MMORPG raids, MOBA matches, tactical shooter squads with voice coordination).
Virtual / Low Interdependence: Online parallel play ("alone together" dynamics, viewing global leaderboards, asynchronous bulletin boards, peripheral spectating).
RT: Task-Dependent Coordination; Peripheral Sociality; Ambient Co-Presence.
OP (Operational Indicators):
Spatial location of players (co-located physical room vs. remote IP network connection).
Structural task coupling (game mechanics requiring joint inputs/simultaneous problem-solving vs. games where individual performance is uncoupled from peers).
IN (Measurement Instruments):
Networked Minds Social Presence Inventory.
Spatial Telepresence Questionnaires.
CS (Core Studies): Kaye (2021); Gonçalves et al. (2023).

Across the articles in your notebook, human-to-machine interaction—involving artificial intelligence (AI), non-player characters (NPCs), computer-controlled agents, robots, and automated chatbots—is addressed through several distinct operational frameworks and experimental paradigms:
1. The Theoretical Framing: "Social in the Outcomes"
In their systematic review of 263 publications, Gonçalves et al. (2023) note that while some researchers define social gaming strictly as "non-solitary" human play, a substantial body of work defines social gaming by whether it produces social outcomes
. Under this conceptualization:
Artificial Social Entities as Co-Players: Literature in this category argues that as soon as an artificial social entity or bot is introduced into gameplay, fundamental social-psychological dynamics are activated—including evaluation apprehension, social learning, and emotional contagion
.
NPCs and Solitary Sociality: Even single-player gaming or interaction with NPCs can be operationalized as having a social facet because it generates conversational topics, shared cultural capital, and feelings of social connection or shared achievement
.
Artificial Partners as a Social Role: Gonçalves et al. explicitly classify the "artificial partner" (identified in 3.4% of the reviewed studies) as a recognized social gaming role alongside traditional co-players, spectators, and mediators
.
2. Operationalizing Human vs. Computer Agents (Psychophysiology and Flow)
A prominent cluster of empirical studies specifically operationalizes human-to-machine gameplay by comparing it directly against human-to-human play
:
Psychological & Physiological Discrepancies: As reviewed by Kaye (2021), experimental studies systematically show that human co-players elicit significantly stronger effects than computer-controlled agents across perceived social presence, positive emotions, physiological arousal, and flow
.
Perceptions of "Humanness" & Competence: Studies exploring human–AI collaboration (such as Ashktorab et al., 2020; Wehbe et al., 2017; Bowman & Banks, 2019) measure how players evaluate machine partners on dimensions of perceived intelligence, likeability, creativity, empathy, and rapport
. Players consistently use more positive descriptors and report higher rapport when they believe their partner possesses human agency rather than automated programming
.
3. Deception and "Simulated Group Experience" Paradigms
To isolate the psychological effect of perceived sociality from actual human responsiveness, researchers frequently operationalize machine play through experimental deception
:
Machine Agents Disguised as Humans: In multiple controlled laboratory studies, participants play alone against or alongside AI algorithms while being told they are interacting with another human over a network
. This tests whether the mere belief in a human co-player is sufficient to induce social presence and competitive/cooperative arousal
.
Humans Disguised as Machine Agents: Conversely, other protocols lead players to believe their partner is an AI or bot when they are actually paired with a human confederate, allowing researchers to measure how anti-bot biases or perceptions of artificiality depress feelings of affiliation
.
4. Computer-Controlled Play as a Non-Social Baseline Control
In clinical and cognitive trials, human-to-machine play is frequently operationalized as the definitional opposite of social interaction
:
The Go Game Randomized Controlled Trial (Iizuka et al., 2019): The researchers explicitly designed the control group ("Non-face-to-face group") around solitary tablet gameplay against an automated computer application
. By keeping the strategic game rules identical between conditions, playing against the machine served as the experimental contrast to isolate what in-person verbal exchanges, tactile board feedback, and reading an opponent's facial expressions contributed to cognitive outcomes
.
5. Chatbots and AI as Automated Social Mediators
Finally, human-to-machine mechanics are operationalized not just as opponents or teammates, but as automated facilitators of interpersonal connection
:
Chatbot Facilitators in Digital Interventions (Janssen et al., 2023): In the SamenSpelen mobile app, an automated chatbot acts as an algorithmic facilitator within group chats
. The machine introduces game rules, delivers conversational prompts, and serves as an automated "conversation starter" to scaffold memories, photo sharing (PhotoSnake), and reciprocal dialogue between older adults and younger family members
.
Social Chatbots as Surrogates for Friendship (Latikka et al., 2025): Recent survey research expands this to evaluate standalone conversational AI agents (social chatbots), examining how using machines as relational surrogates correlates with psychological distress, loneliness, and self-esteem across different age groups
.
